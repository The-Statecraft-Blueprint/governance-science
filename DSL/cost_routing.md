# Cost Routing

**Status**: Draft  
**Category**: Foundational Concept  
**Version**: 1.0  
**Last Updated**: April 2026

---

## Definition

The structural property of a governance mechanism that determines where consequences of decisions accrue—specifically, whether costs and benefits flow back to decision-makers (strong cost routing) or to other actors, other times, or other jurisdictions (weak cost routing).

---

## Context

**Good design routes costs back to the decision-maker.**

This principle is not a moral claim about fairness. It's an engineering observation: systems where decision-makers bear the costs of their decisions are self-correcting. Systems where decision-makers are insulated from costs accumulate dysfunction.

When a cook eats their own cooking, feedback is immediate and unambiguous. When a legislator passes an unfunded mandate whose costs fall on implementing agencies and future taxpayers, there is no feedback signal—the legislator faces the same consequences whether the mandate was well-designed or not.

Cost routing describes this structural property. Strong cost routing creates feedback loops. Weak cost routing severs them. The costs don't disappear—they're borne by someone, somewhere, at some time. Weak cost routing just ensures that someone isn't the decision-maker.

This connects directly to degeneracy: weak cost routing creates high degeneracy in accountability relationships. Multiple decision qualities (good mandate, bad mandate) produce the same consequence for the decision-maker (no cost). The system cannot distinguish between inputs.

Cost routing applies across scales:
- **Individual**: Market entrepreneurs bear the costs of bad products
- **Organizational**: Executives with equity bear the costs of bad strategy  
- **Municipal**: Residents who vote on bonds bear debt service costs
- **National**: Legislators in competitive districts bear electoral costs of bad votes
- **Intergenerational**: Often broken—current decision-makers externalize costs to future generations

The claim "the costs are already being borne" reflects reality. Weak cost routing doesn't eliminate costs; it shifts them. The structural question is whether the feedback loop to the decision-maker is intact.

## Example

**Example 1: Unfunded Mandates**

Congress passes legislation requiring states to implement a new program but provides no funding. The mandate may be excellent policy or terrible policy—Congress receives no feedback either way.

The costs exist. State budgets absorb them, other programs get cut, taxpayers fund them, or service quality suffers. But Congress doesn't bear them. Weak cost routing.

If the mandate included automatic federal funding tied to compliance costs, Congress would face budget consequences from mandate scope. Decision quality would affect the decision-maker. Stronger cost routing.

**Example 2: Deficit Spending vs. Pay-Go Constraints**

Standard deficit spending routes costs to future taxpayers: current legislators get credit for spending, future generations bear debt service. The decision-maker (current legislator) is insulated from costs. Weak cost routing.

Pay-As-You-Go (PAYGO) constraints require that new spending be offset by cuts elsewhere or revenue increases. The decision-maker must acknowledge costs in the present. The feedback loop is partially restored. Stronger cost routing.

**Example 3: Professional Licensing**

Medical licensing with malpractice liability routes costs of incompetent practice back to practitioners: insurance premiums rise, licenses can be revoked, reputation suffers. Decision quality (clinical competence) affects decision-maker (practitioner). Strong cost routing.

A licensing regime with no enforcement, no liability, and no reputation effects would have weak cost routing—patients bear costs of incompetence, practitioners don't.

## Trade-offs

**Benefits:**
- Strong cost routing creates self-correcting systems with functional feedback loops
- Decision-makers internalize the full consequences of their choices
- Reduces need for external enforcement—consequences are automatic
- Creates structural selection pressure for decision quality
- Aligns individual incentives with system outcomes

**Costs:**
- Strong cost routing may be impossible for some decisions (intergenerational, cross-jurisdictional)
- May reduce risk-taking when some risk is desirable (innovation, entrepreneurship)
- Can create excessive short-termism if only immediate costs route back
- Measurement and attribution are often difficult (which decision caused which cost?)
- May conflict with other values (solidarity, risk-pooling, collective responsibility)

**Design Tensions:**
- Individual accountability vs. collective action: Cost routing to individuals may undermine collective efforts
- Immediate feedback vs. long-term effects: Some costs only manifest over time
- Attribution clarity vs. complex causation: Real-world costs often have multiple causes
- Accountability vs. insurance: Risk-pooling deliberately weakens individual cost routing

## Related Terms

**Directly Related:**
- **Degeneracy** - Weak cost routing creates high degeneracy; multiple decision qualities produce same consequence for decision-maker
- **Causal Determinism** - Strong cost routing with high determinism means decisions reliably and distinguishably produce consequences
- **Wrong-Pocket Problem** - Specific structural failure where costs accrue to different actor/jurisdiction/time than benefits

**Contrasts With:**
- **Externalities** - Economic term for costs borne by parties external to transaction; cost routing is the governance architecture perspective on same phenomenon

**Builds On:**
- **Structural Selection Pressure** - Cost routing creates selection pressure; strong routing selects for decision quality
- **Accountability Mechanisms** - Function only when costs route back to create feedback

## Common Misuses

**Weaponized Form:**
"Decision-makers should bear costs" used to oppose all risk-pooling, insurance, or collective responsibility. Some cost-sharing is legitimate and valuable. The question is whether weak cost routing serves a purpose (risk-sharing, collective action) or merely insulates from accountability.

**Common Confusion:**
Conflating cost routing with *punishment*. Cost routing is about feedback loops, not retribution. A well-designed system doesn't punish decision-makers—it ensures they receive accurate information about consequences so they can adjust. Framing cost routing as punishment misunderstands its function.

**Over-Application:**
Assuming all costs can and should route back to identifiable decision-makers. Some decisions genuinely have diffuse causation. Some costs are legitimately socialized. Some time horizons exceed any decision-maker's tenure. Perfect cost routing is neither possible nor always desirable.

## Measurement

**Indicators:**
- Do decision-makers face different outcomes based on decision quality?
- How long is the causal chain between decision and consequence for the decision-maker?
- What proportion of total costs falls on the decision-maker vs. others?
- Are costs contemporaneous with decisions or delayed?

**Evaluation Questions:**
- If this decision is good vs. bad, who experiences the difference?
- What would the decision-maker's consequence be if they decided optimally vs. poorly?
- Where do the costs actually land, and how long until they land there?
- What structural features connect decision to consequence?

**Warning Signs:**
- Decision-makers consistently avoid consequences of decisions (bailouts, term limits, jurisdiction-shifting)
- Costs are systematically borne by future generations, other jurisdictions, or unorganized constituencies
- High-quality and low-quality decisions produce same outcomes for decision-maker
- Significant time delay between decision and consequence materialization

## Historical Notes

Cost routing synthesizes insights from multiple intellectual traditions:

**Public Choice Theory** (Buchanan, Tullock): Analyzed how institutional structures affect political incentives, including how decision-makers can externalize costs to diffuse constituencies.

**Institutional Economics** (North, Ostrom): Studied how institutional rules create incentive structures that shape behavior, including feedback mechanisms.

**Mechanism Design** (Hurwicz, Maskin, Myerson): Formalized how to design systems where individual incentives align with social outcomes—which requires that actors face consequences of their choices.

The synthesis into "cost routing" as a governance architecture term emphasizes the structural property of mechanisms: where do costs go? This is prior to normative questions about whether that distribution is fair or efficient. First, describe the structure. Then evaluate it.
