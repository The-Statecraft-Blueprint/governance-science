# Structure

**Status**: Draft  
**Category**: Foundational Concept  
**Version**: 1.0  
**Last Updated**: May 2026

## Definition

The institutional rules, procedures, incentive arrangements, and architectural constraints that determine *how* collective decisions are made, how power is allocated, and how costs and benefits are distributed — as distinct from the specific policy decisions made within those arrangements (the *what*), or the individual actors who operate them (the *who*).

---

## Context

The most important distinction in governance science is between structure and everything else. Structure is the architecture of decision-making itself — not what a government decides, not who decides it, but the rules and arrangements that shape how decisions get made regardless of content or personnel.

This distinction matters because most governance failures are structural. When the same dysfunction recurs across different administrations, different parties, and different individuals, the failure is not attributable to the people. It is attributable to the architecture those people are operating within. Structure shapes behavior the way gravity shapes physics: it is not a moral force, not a personal one, but a design feature of the system that produces predictable outcomes regardless of who is in it.

**Structure vs. Policy (the How vs. What distinction)**: Policy is what government decides — healthcare coverage, tax rates, environmental standards, foreign commitments. These are legitimately contested by people with different values and priorities. Structure is how government decides — how committees are organized, how votes are recorded and attributed, how budgets are constructed, how accountability flows between branches. Structure questions are design questions. They have better and worse answers that can be evaluated on engineering criteria independent of ideological preference.

This distinction is the load-bearing idea in governance science. A structural reform changes how the decision-making process works while leaving what decisions get made entirely to democratic deliberation. A policy reform changes what decisions get made within an existing process. TSB and the governance-science project are concerned exclusively with structural reform. The boundary is the test: "Does this change how government operates, or what it does?"

**Structure vs. Personnel**: Structural analysis asks what the architecture produces regardless of who operates within it. When a system repeatedly produces the same outcomes despite personnel change — gridlock, captured responsiveness, expanding executive power — this is structural evidence, not a personnel finding. The people within the system are responding rationally to the incentives the structure creates. Replacing them with different people operating within the same structure produces the same incentives and, predictably, the same outcomes. "Better systems, not better people" is the public-facing version of this principle.

**Structure vs. Culture and Norms**: Culture and norms are informal behavioral regularities — shared expectations, conventions, and traditions that shape behavior without formal enforcement. They interact with structure but are distinct from it. Norms have high [observer dependency](observer_dependency.md): they function only as long as enough actors choose to observe them, and collapse when a determined actor defects. Structural mechanisms have lower observer dependency: they function through enforcement rather than choice. The relationship between structure and norms is critical for governance design: norms that appear to constrain behavior are not structural constraints. When they are load-bearing, they need to be converted to structural mechanisms.

**What structure includes**: Constitutional provisions, statutory rules and procedures, committee organization, budget process architecture, voting rules, accountability mechanisms with enforcement, term limits with legal consequence, appointment and confirmation procedures, and the designed incentive arrangements that reward or penalize particular behaviors regardless of who is in office.

**What structure does not include**: Policy content, partisan alignment, personnel decisions, cultural norms and conventions, informal expectations, and the values or intentions of individual actors. These interact with structure — cultural norms can reinforce or undermine structural mechanisms, and personnel make discretionary choices within structural constraints — but they are not part of the structure itself.

## Example

**Example 1: Congressional Voting Procedures — The How/What Distinction**

Before 1970, most House votes were unrecorded teller votes — members walked past a counter and no individual record was kept. In 1970, the Legislative Reorganization Act required recorded votes and made them inexpensive to request. This was a structural change: it changed how voting transparency worked, not what any legislation said.

The structural change produced predictable behavioral consequences regardless of which party was in power, which legislators were serving, or what policies were being considered: recorded votes made it possible for concentrated interests to track and punish individual legislators in real time, while constituents retained only the election as a response mechanism. The structure shaped the behavior. Neither party designed this outcome; the architecture produced it.

**Example 2: Budget Process — Structure vs. Policy**

Whether the federal government should spend more or less on defense, healthcare, or infrastructure is a policy question with legitimate ideological disagreement. How the budget process works — whether automatic continuing resolutions exist, how reconciliation is structured, what triggers a shutdown — is a structural question with better and worse design answers independent of what the budget ultimately contains. Governance science addresses the second question, not the first.

**Example 3: The 22nd Amendment — Structure Replacing Norm**

The two-term limit was a norm for 150 years — a convention held in place by shared expectations, not structural enforcement. When the norm broke, Congress converted it to structure: a constitutional amendment with no discretion in its application. The structural version functions regardless of what any president wants, believes, or argues. The norm version functioned only as long as presidents chose to observe it. Same constraint, radically different structural properties.

## Trade-offs

**Benefits:**
- The structure/policy distinction makes structural reform politically tractable across ideological lines — people who disagree on what government should do can agree on how it should function
- Structural analysis identifies durable solutions rather than personnel-dependent ones
- Structural mechanisms are more predictable than norms — their effects can be anticipated and designed for

**Costs:**
- Structural mechanisms are harder to build and modify than norms or policies — they often require legislative action, constitutional amendment, or significant institutional redesign
- Structure can constrain beneficial flexibility as well as harmful abuse — well-designed architecture must distinguish between constraints that prevent dysfunction and constraints that prevent adaptation
- The structure/policy boundary is genuinely ambiguous in some cases — procedural rules can have policy effects, and some policy choices embed structural assumptions

**Design Tensions:**
- Constraint vs. flexibility: Good structural design prevents dysfunction without eliminating the discretion that legitimate governance requires
- Formality vs. adaptability: More formal structural mechanisms have lower observer dependency but less capacity to adapt to novel circumstances
- The how/what boundary: Some questions that look structural are actually policy questions in disguise, and vice versa; governance architects must be able to distinguish them

## Related Terms

**Directly Related:**
- [**Observer Dependency**](observer_dependency.md) — A key property of structural mechanisms: how much does the mechanism depend on continuous belief, attention, or maintenance to function?
- [**Structural Drift**](structural_drift.md) — What happens to structural mechanisms when they are architecturally permissive: each successive actor shapes them further, regardless of original intent
- [**Dominant Extractive Pattern**](dominant_extractive_pattern.md) — A systemic property that emerges from structural arrangements that systematically route costs to the majority and benefits to the minority near power
- [**Degeneracy**](degeneracy.md) — A property of structural accountability mechanisms: how well does the structure distinguish between different decision qualities?
- [**Consequence Alignment**](../patterns/consequence_alignment.md) — The design pattern for ensuring structure routes consequences appropriately back to decision-makers

**Contrasts With:**
- **Policy** — What government decides within a structure, as opposed to how the decision-making process works; governance science addresses structure, not policy
- **Personnel** — Who operates within a structure, as opposed to the structure itself; structural analysis holds personnel constant and examines what the architecture produces

**Builds On:**
- **Governance Architecture** — The broader framework within which structure is defined: the full set of institutional rules, arrangements, and mechanisms that shape collective decision-making

## Common Misuses

**"We need to change the structure of [policy area]":**
Commonly used to mean changing the content of policy in a particular domain — "the structure of healthcare" usually refers to insurance arrangements, coverage requirements, or payment models, not to the decision-making procedures through which healthcare policy is set. This is policy language, not structural language. Structure in governance science refers to the decision-making process, not the content of decisions.

**Treating personnel change as structural reform:**
"Electing better people" or "appointing different officials" is personnel change. If the architecture produces the same incentives and outcomes regardless of who operates within it, personnel change is not structural reform. Structural reform changes the architecture. The test: would this change produce different outcomes if the same people remained in place? If no, it is structural. If only when different people are in place, it is personnel.

**Calling norms "structural":**
"We have structural norms against..." conflates high observer dependency conventions with structural mechanisms. Norms are not structural in the governance science sense — they are behavioral regularities that function through collective choice, not through design. When norms are load-bearing, they must be converted to structural mechanisms.

## Measurement

**Indicators of structural vs. non-structural:**
- Does the mechanism function the same way regardless of who is in charge? (Structural) Or does it depend on who's in charge choosing to observe it? (Norm)
- Would the outcome change if different people with different values operated within the same arrangement? If yes, the arrangement is structural — it's shaping the behavior. If only personnel matter, the arrangement may be weak structurally.
- Is the constraint enforced automatically or through discretionary action? Automatic enforcement is structural. Discretionary enforcement depends on the values of the enforcer.

**Evaluation Questions:**
- Is this a question about how decisions get made, or what decisions get made? (How = structural; What = policy)
- Does the dysfunction persist across personnel change? If yes, look for structural causes.
- What would change if we removed this arrangement? If behavior would change regardless of who the actors are, the arrangement is structural.

**Warning Signs of Structural Misdiagnosis:**
- "We just need better people in office" — treating a structural problem as a personnel problem
- "We've always done it this way" — describing a norm as if it were a structural mechanism
- "Both parties agreed to..." — describing a norm, not a constraint
- "The system is working" when the same dysfunctional outcomes persist across administrations — the system may be working exactly as structured

## Historical Notes

The how vs. what distinction is the definitional axis of governance science as a field. It is implicit in the work of institutional economists (North's distinction between rules of the game and plays of the game), in constitutional theory (process vs. substance), and in systems engineering (architecture vs. implementation). Governance science makes this distinction explicit and central, using it as the formal boundary of the field's scope.

The term "structure" in this sense draws on but is more specific than its use in social science, where "structure" often refers to social arrangements broadly. In governance science, structure is specifically the designed institutional architecture of decision-making — the formal rules and incentive arrangements that shape how collective choices are made, enforced, and revised.
