# Dominant Extractive Pattern

**Status**: Draft  
**Category**: Systemic Property  
**Version**: 1.0  
**Last Updated**: May 2026

---

## Definition

A system exhibits a **Dominant Extractive Pattern** when its operational architecture systematically routes costs, burdens, or exclusions to the majority of affected participants while concentrating benefits among a minority with proximity to power — and when this routing is structural rather than incidental, meaning the system cannot achieve its stated purpose for all eligible participants even under optimal conditions.

**The Dominant Pattern Rule**: When 60% or more of a system's operational pattern is extractive, the minority that achieves legitimate outcomes does not constitute evidence that the system is working. The dominant pattern is the design. Exceptions do not offset the architecture.

---

## Context

The most persistent confusion in institutional analysis is the distinction between a system failing and a system working as designed. A system that routes outcomes toward a concentrated minority while imposing costs on a diffuse majority may be performing exactly as its architecture specifies. The problem is not malfunction. The problem is design.

The Dominant Pattern Rule provides a formal threshold for this distinction. Below 60%, a system's extractive outcomes might be attributable to edge cases, bad actors within an otherwise functional architecture, or implementation failures that better personnel or stronger enforcement could correct. Above 60%, the dominant operational pattern is extractive — the architecture itself produces the extraction regardless of personnel quality, intent, or enforcement. At this threshold, the system's stated purpose and its operational design have diverged structurally, not incidentally.

**"Privileged calibrated access"** is the formal term for the legitimizing narrative that systems exhibiting dominant extractive patterns typically maintain. The system appears to offer universal access — technically, anyone can participate; technically, all citizens have representation; technically, all applicants are eligible. But access is calibrated by design to produce differential outcomes. The calibration is the design, not a departure from it. The appearance of universality serves as institutional cover: "See, we're not excluding anyone."

This legitimizing narrative is the hardest aspect of the dominant extractive pattern to diagnose precisely because it is technically accurate. Citizens do have representation. Applications are accepted. The process is formally open. The structural analysis must get underneath the formal description to the operational architecture: where do the costs actually go? Where do the benefits actually concentrate? What proportion of the operational pattern routes toward extraction? The answers to these questions reveal whether universal access is genuine or calibrated.

**The production process principle**: A critical insight from systems analysis is that changing the actors within a system does not change the production process if the production process is architectural. Traditional analysis focused on actors — who holds power, who owns the institution, who makes decisions — tends to miss dominant extractive patterns because such patterns persist regardless of who the actors are. The architecture routes outcomes regardless of the values, intentions, or identities of the people operating within it. A system where 65% of the operational pattern is extractive will continue exhibiting that pattern after the actors change, because the pattern is in the process, not the personnel.

This is the structural version of TSB's core principle — "better systems, not better people" — stated as a diagnostic criterion: when a system exhibits a dominant extractive pattern, personnel replacement is insufficient. The production process must be redesigned.

**The design implication**: A system exhibiting a dominant extractive pattern cannot be repaired at the margins. Adding accountability mechanisms, increasing transparency, electing different people, or improving enforcement of existing rules treats the exceptions (the 40% or less of outcomes that serve the stated purpose) as the standard case and the dominant pattern as the exception to be corrected. This misdiagnoses the system. At above 60% extraction, the dominant pattern is the design. Correction requires architectural redesign, not operational adjustment.

## Example

**Example 1: Congressional Responsiveness — Legislative Coercion Loop (United States)**

Members of Congress report spending 30+ hours per week in call centers dialing wealthy donors. Party leadership enforces donor responsiveness through committee assignments, floor scheduling, primary support, and campaign funding. The architecture routes legislators' time, attention, and policy responsiveness toward concentrated donor interests by design.

Applying the Dominant Pattern Rule: the majority of how the legislative process operates routes responsiveness away from constituents and toward concentrated interests. The legislators who genuinely serve constituents primarily are the exceptions — the 40% or less that the dominant pattern permits through. Their existence does not constitute evidence that the system is working as intended for all constituents. It constitutes evidence that the dominant pattern is not total, which is different.

The stated purpose of the legislature — representing constituents — cannot be achieved for all constituents under this architecture even under optimal conditions, because the operational architecture routes responsiveness toward concentrated interests by design. The 30+ hours spent dialing donors is not a bug; it is what the architecture produces.

The legitimizing narrative (privileged calibrated access): any constituent can vote, contact their representative, and attend town halls. Access is technically universal. But it is calibrated by design to produce differential responsiveness based on the ability to provide campaign resources. The formal universality provides cover for the structural calibration.

*Related: See LRA §§120–121 analysis in [Actionable Transparency](../patterns/actionable_transparency.md) for the specific architectural mechanism producing this pattern.*

**Example 2: Campaign Finance Disclosure — Asymmetric Access (United States)**

Campaign finance disclosure requirements are formally universal — all donors above threshold must be disclosed, all disclosures are public record. But the operational architecture of disclosure systems (format, searchability, usability) is calibrated to produce differential outcomes: well-resourced actors can navigate and act on disclosures in real time; ordinary voters cannot do so on the timeline that would make the information matter.

The dominant pattern routes the effective use of transparency toward concentrated interests. The formal universality of disclosure provides the legitimizing narrative: "all information is public." The structural calibration produces the actual outcome: effective access is privileged.

*Related: See [Actionable Transparency](../patterns/actionable_transparency.md) pattern and [Asymmetric Transparency](../patterns/actionable_transparency.md) anti-pattern.*

## Trade-offs

**Benefits (of the Dominant Pattern Rule as diagnostic criterion):**
- Provides a formal threshold that distinguishes structural failures from incidental ones
- Directs analytical attention toward the production process rather than individual actors
- Makes the "better systems, not better people" diagnosis precise and testable
- Identifies when marginal reform is insufficient vs. when architectural redesign is required

**Costs (of applying the Dominant Pattern Rule):**
- The 60% threshold is a useful heuristic, not a precisely derivable constant — empirical measurement of what proportion of a system's pattern is extractive is methodologically complex
- May categorize systems as exhibiting dominant extractive patterns even when extractive outcomes are partially attributable to implementation failures rather than architectural design
- Risk of overapplication: not every system with above-60% bad outcomes has an extractive architecture — some are simply underfunded, understaffed, or recently degraded

**Design Tensions:**
- Accuracy vs. parsimony: A rigorous determination of whether a system exhibits a dominant extractive pattern requires mapping where costs and benefits actually go, which is analytically demanding
- Reform vs. replacement: Identifying a dominant extractive pattern argues for architectural redesign, but incremental reform is often the only politically tractable path; the diagnostic should inform strategy, not foreclose it

## Related Terms

**Directly Related:**
- [**Consequence Alignment**](../patterns/consequence_alignment.md) — The pattern whose absence most directly enables dominant extractive patterns; when decision-makers do not bear the costs of the systems they design, the architecture can route extraction to others indefinitely
- [**Actionable Transparency**](../patterns/actionable_transparency.md) — Asymmetric transparency (the anti-pattern) is a mechanism through which dominant extractive patterns maintain legitimizing narratives; "privileged calibrated access" is operationalized through asymmetric access to disclosed information
- [**Structural Drift**](structural_drift.md) — Dominant extractive patterns tend to amplify through structural drift; each successive administration that operates within an extractive architecture shapes it further in the direction the architecture already favors
- [**Cost Routing**](cost_routing.md) — Dominant extractive patterns are characterized by systematic weak cost routing to the majority

**Contrasts With:**
- **Incidental Extraction** — Below the 60% threshold, extractive outcomes may be attributable to implementation failures, edge cases, or bad actors within an otherwise functional architecture; the Dominant Pattern Rule distinguishes structural from incidental
- **Regulatory Capture** — A specific mechanism by which an agency's decision-making becomes aligned with regulated industry rather than the public; regulatory capture can be a component of a dominant extractive pattern but is narrower in scope

**Builds On:**
- [**Degeneracy**](degeneracy.md) — Dominant extractive patterns often involve high degeneracy: the accountability mechanism cannot distinguish between decisions that serve the stated purpose and decisions that serve the dominant extractive pattern

## Common Misuses

**The Exception Fallacy:**
Pointing to instances where the system produces its stated purpose (the 40% or less of outcomes that pass through) as evidence that the system is functioning correctly. The Dominant Pattern Rule specifically addresses this: when the dominant pattern is extractive, exceptions confirm that the pattern is not total — not that the architecture is sound. A system where most people are excluded but some make it through is not a working system with edge cases. It is an extraction system with a legitimizing minority.

**Conflating with corruption:**
Dominant extractive patterns do not require corrupt actors, bad intent, or individual wrongdoing. The pattern is architectural — it operates through the production process regardless of who operates within it. Framing dominant extractive patterns as corruption focuses analysis on individual actors and misses the structural diagnosis. The system may be producing extraction through the fully legal, fully intentional, and entirely good-faith behavior of every person within it.

**Actor substitution as remedy:**
The most common policy error following a dominant extractive pattern diagnosis is replacing the people operating the system — electing different leaders, appointing different administrators, hiring different managers. Changing the actors does not change the production process. If the architecture routes 65% of outcomes toward extraction, new actors operating within the same architecture will produce the same dominant pattern. The diagnosis calls for architectural redesign, not personnel replacement.

**Over-extension to policy disagreement:**
Not every policy outcome that disadvantages a majority is a dominant extractive pattern. Tax policy, regulatory decisions, and public spending involve legitimate distributional disagreements where the architecture may be functioning correctly while producing outcomes that some find unjust. The Dominant Pattern Rule applies to the operational architecture — how the system processes inputs and routes costs and benefits — not to the content of policy outputs that result from legitimate political decisions.

## Measurement

**Indicators:**
- Mapping actual cost and benefit flows: where do the costs of system operation actually accrue? Where do the benefits actually concentrate?
- Proportion of eligible participants who achieve the system's stated purpose under normal operating conditions
- Whether outcomes change substantially when actors change, or persist regardless of personnel
- Whether the legitimizing narrative of universal access is borne out by operational analysis of actual access

**Evaluation Questions:**
- What proportion of the system's operational pattern routes toward extraction rather than the stated purpose?
- Can the system achieve its stated purpose for all eligible participants simultaneously, or does the architecture structurally prevent this even under optimal conditions?
- What is the legitimizing narrative, and does operational analysis confirm or contradict it?
- What would it take to change the dominant pattern — different people, or different architecture?

**The Dominant Pattern Rule Test:**
Does above 60% of how the system actually operates route costs to the majority and benefits to the minority with proximity to power? If yes, personnel replacement is insufficient. Architectural redesign is required.

**Warning Signs:**
- "Yes, the process is difficult for some, but look at the success stories" — Exception Fallacy in operation
- The formal description of the system (universal access, equal treatment) significantly diverges from operational analysis of where costs and benefits actually go
- System outcomes persist through multiple personnel changes across administrations or leadership transitions
- The legitimizing narrative is more prominent in official communications than operational evidence

## Historical Notes

The Dominant Extractive Pattern and Dominant Pattern Rule were formalized by Clinton Alden in the KOSMOS MRF (Mapped Reality Framework) as tools for analyzing systems where conventional institutional analysis fails to identify structural extraction because it focuses on individual actors rather than the production process. Alden's insight — that systems can be working exactly as designed while simultaneously failing their stated purpose for the majority of participants — provides the formal basis for distinguishing architectural extraction from incidental poor outcomes.

The concept adapts Alden's framework for governance architecture application, where the production process is the institutional architecture (rules, procedures, incentive structures) that routes political responsiveness, policy outcomes, and resource allocation. The governance application emphasizes the "privileged calibrated access" concept — the formal description that allows systems to maintain legitimacy while structurally preventing universal access — and the design implication that dominant extractive patterns require architectural redesign, not operational adjustment.

The insight that changing actors does not change the production process parallels and extends TSB's "better systems, not better people" principle: when the architecture is extractive, the diagnostic is structural and the remedy is structural.
