# Observer Dependency / Intrinsic Stability

**Status**: Draft  
**Category**: Systemic Property  
**Version**: 1.0  
**Last Updated**: May 2026

---

## Definition

**Observer Dependency** is the property of a governance mechanism that requires continuous conscious participation, belief, or active maintenance from participants to function — the system persists only as long as enough actors choose to observe it, and degrades or collapses when that active maintenance is withdrawn.

**Intrinsic Stability** is the complementary design goal: a governance mechanism that functions correctly regardless of who is in charge, who is watching, or what any individual participant believes about it — because its operation is congruent with the structural requirements of the system, not dependent on the good intentions or heroic effort of those within it.

These are paired concepts. Observer Dependency names the failure mode; Intrinsic Stability names the design target.

---

## Context

Democratic systems rely on a mix of structural mechanisms and informal conventions. Structural mechanisms — constitutional provisions, statutory rules, institutional procedures with enforcement — function whether or not participants choose to honor them. Informal conventions — norms, traditions, gentleman's agreements, shared expectations — function only as long as participants choose to observe them.

The difference matters structurally because norms are high observer dependency structures. When a single actor defects from a norm, the norm may collapse — and because no enforcement mechanism exists, the defection becomes the new baseline. What appeared to be a constraint was actually a behavioral equilibrium that required continuous collective maintenance to sustain. The moment the maintenance stopped, the constraint disappeared.

A useful diagnostic criterion, formalized by Eric Whitney in a different domain: **a pattern that collapses without continuous effort, management, or suppression is by definition not part of the original design — it is an installation running over it.** Applied to governance: a democratic norm that collapses the moment a single actor defects is not structural architecture. It is a behavioral convention masquerading as a load-bearing wall.

The deeper principle, which Whitney articulates from a clinical framework and governance architecture arrives at independently: **original design does not require enforcement because it is congruent with the underlying architecture.** A governance system functioning correctly does not require heroic individuals, continuous norm reinforcement, or exceptional circumstances. If it does, that is a design finding — not a personnel problem, not a cultural failure, but a structural diagnosis indicating that the system is running above its enforcement dependency threshold.

This connects to the **Enforcement Dependency** diagnostic: when a system requires continuous effort just to maintain basic function — constant enforcement, ongoing cultural maintenance, active suppression of defection — it is operating above the threshold at which structural intervention is warranted. The system is not failing. It is working exactly as its design permits, which is the problem.

The public-facing version of this principle — "democracy shouldn't require heroic effort" — is the output of this structural analysis. The engineering principle underneath it: democracy designed with low observer dependency functions regardless of who is in charge or what they believe. Democracy designed with high observer dependency is one defection away from structural failure.

The **painting lines vs. pouring concrete** distinction is the preventive form of this concept. Painting lines (norms, conventions, informal expectations) creates high observer dependency structures — they require continuous attention and collective belief to function. Pouring concrete (structural mechanisms with enforcement, constitutional provisions, automatic consequences) creates lower observer dependency structures that function regardless of participant behavior.

The 22nd Amendment is the canonical governance example. For over 150 years, the two-term limit was a norm — George Washington established it, and subsequent presidents observed it. The norm appeared load-bearing until a single actor defected. Congress then had to convert the norm to concrete: a constitutional amendment with no enforcement discretion. The observer dependency was exposed by defection and reduced by structural redesign.

**The design implication**: every governance mechanism should be evaluated for its observer dependency level. Mechanisms with high observer dependency are fragile by design — not because of the people within them, but because the architecture requires continuous maintenance from those people to sustain what looks like structural function. Designing toward intrinsic stability means building mechanisms that function correctly even when participants are self-interested, inattentive, or actively hostile.

## Example

**Example 1: Presidential Norm Cascade (United States)**

Presidential candidates releasing tax returns was a norm — not a legal requirement — established after Nixon. It functioned for decades because each successive candidate chose to observe it. When a single candidate defected, the norm collapsed: no enforcement mechanism existed, the defection created precedent, and the convention evaporated within a single election cycle.

High observer dependency: norm required continuous collective choice to observe. One defection → collapse. Design implication: the information the norm was meant to produce requires a structural requirement, not a convention.

**Example 2: The 22nd Amendment (United States, 1951)**

The two-term limit was a norm from 1797 until 1940. When the norm broke, Congress converted it to a constitutional amendment. The amendment does not require anyone to believe in it or observe it: a president who attempts a third term is simply ineligible. The constraint functions regardless of the president's intentions or supporters' preferences.

Lower observer dependency: constitutional mechanism with no enforcement discretion. The structural redesign reduced the observer dependency of a critical democratic constraint from high to low.

**Example 3: The Federal Reserve (United States)**

Monetary policy mechanisms — interest rate targets, reserve requirements, open market operations — function regardless of whether any individual believes in them or approves of their outcomes on a given day. The Federal Reserve's operational architecture produces its outputs through structural mechanisms, not through the continuous belief maintenance of participants.

This is low observer dependency by design. The original "Refactoring Government" proposal for a Governance Design Agency cited the Federal Reserve as a model for institutional independence; the structural reason the comparison is apt is that Federal Reserve mechanisms have intrinsic stability — they function without requiring heroic effort or continuous belief from anyone.

**Example 4: Clinical Parallel — Whitney's Observer's Collapse Function**

Eric Whitney, an osteopathic physician, independently formalized a parallel principle at human scale through the Observer's Collapse Function (OCF). In clinical practice, Whitney identifies the distinction between a patient's "original design" (patterns congruent with their underlying biological architecture, requiring no enforcement to sustain) and "installation" (patterns running over original design, requiring continuous effort, management, or suppression to maintain).

Whitney's Enforcement Dependency variable operationalizes the threshold: a patient whose functional identity requires continuous enforcement — whose self-concept collapses under stress, whose patterns require constant management to sustain — is operating above 0.7 enforcement dependency, the clinical intervention marker. The 92% collapse correlation above this threshold makes the diagnostic structural: not a judgment, a physics-grounded finding.

Whitney's formalization validates the governance principle from a different domain: a system requiring continuous enforcement to maintain basic function is not a well-designed system — it is an installation. Original design (in governance: well-designed structural mechanisms; in clinical medicine: patterns congruent with biological architecture) does not require enforcement because it is congruent with the underlying architecture.

This cross-domain convergence — the same structural principle surfacing independently in institutional design, governance architecture, and clinical medicine — is evidence that observer dependency and intrinsic stability describe a real property of complex adaptive systems, not merely a governance design preference.

## Trade-offs

**Benefits (of designing toward Intrinsic Stability):**
- System functions correctly regardless of personnel quality — no single actor can produce structural failure by defection
- Reduces the heroic effort required of participants — those within the system can act on ordinary incentives and still produce the design intent
- Creates predictable, enforceable constraints that concentrated interests cannot exploit through norm defection
- Enables accountability that doesn't depend on continuous political will to enforce

**Costs (of designing toward Intrinsic Stability):**
- Structural mechanisms are harder to build than norms — require legislative action, constitutional amendment, institutional design
- Lower flexibility — mechanisms with high intrinsic stability resist adaptation as well as defection
- May constrain beneficial discretion — some valuable institutional behavior requires the flexibility that observer dependency provides
- Converting norms to structure can produce unintended rigidity at the edges

**Design Tensions:**
- Flexibility vs. stability: High observer dependency enables adaptive response; low observer dependency enables predictable constraint. Good governance architecture uses both, deliberately, in the right places.
- Discretion vs. enforcement: Professional judgment sometimes requires protection from automatic structural consequence. Designing every mechanism for intrinsic stability would eliminate beneficial discretion.
- Norm maintenance as democratic culture: Some observer dependency may be appropriate — civic participation, democratic culture, and shared norms serve functions beyond their structural content. The design goal is appropriate observer dependency, not zero.

## Related Terms

**Directly Related:**
- [**Structural Drift**](structural_drift.md) — The systemic consequence when high observer dependency norms fail: architectural permissiveness allows successive actors to expand powers that informal constraints were supposed to limit
- [**Causal Determinism**](causal_determinism.md) — Intrinsic stability requires high causal determinism: the mechanism must reliably produce its intended effect regardless of who operates it
- [**Degeneracy**](degeneracy.md) — High observer dependency often produces high degeneracy: when the mechanism depends on participant choice, the same structural form can produce many different outcomes depending on who chooses to observe it

**Contrasts With:**
- **Norm-Based Governance** — Legitimate and valuable, but structurally distinct from mechanisms designed for intrinsic stability; norms are high observer dependency structures that require conversion to concrete when load-bearing function is required

**Builds On:**
- **Structural Selection Pressure** — Intrinsic stability means the mechanism selects for the desired behavior regardless of actor intent, rather than relying on actors to select for it themselves

## Common Misuses

**Conflating norms with structural mechanisms:**
The most common governance design error. Describing a convention as "the norm we all respect" without recognizing its observer dependency creates the illusion of structural constraint where none exists. Norms appear load-bearing until they're tested. Observer dependency analysis asks: what happens when a single actor stops observing?

**Using intrinsic stability to eliminate all discretion:**
Designing every governance mechanism for maximum intrinsic stability would produce a rigid system incapable of adaptation. The goal is matching intrinsic stability to the load-bearing requirement: use structural mechanisms where constraint must be unconditional; preserve discretion where professional judgment is the design feature.

**Treating observer dependency as a character failure:**
"The system depends on people doing the right thing" is often used as a critique of participants rather than a design critique. Observer dependency is a structural property, not a moral finding. A system with high observer dependency isn't failing because of bad actors — it's succeeding exactly as designed, which is the problem.

## Measurement

**Indicators:**
- Does the mechanism function correctly when a single determined actor defects from the expected behavior?
- Does maintaining basic function require continuous effort, vigilance, or norm reinforcement?
- Would the system produce its intended output if participants acted on ordinary self-interest rather than civic virtue?
- Does the mechanism have explicit enforcement that operates automatically, or does enforcement depend on political will?

**Evaluation Questions:**
- What happens to this mechanism when someone stops choosing to observe it? Is the answer "nothing changes" (low observer dependency) or "the convention collapses" (high observer dependency)?
- Is the system functioning because the design compels correct behavior, or because participants are currently choosing correct behavior?
- What would it take to convert the high observer dependency elements of this mechanism to lower observer dependency structure?

**Warning Signs:**
- "We've always done it this way" — describes a norm, not a structural mechanism
- "We're counting on the next administration to honor the spirit of..." — high observer dependency, explicit
- "The system works because good people are in charge" — above enforcement dependency threshold; design intervention warranted
- Single defections producing cascading norm collapse

## Historical Notes

The Observer's Collapse Function (OCF) was formalized by Clinton Alden in the KOSMOS Systems Auditor framework as a tool for analyzing institutional collapse risk. The OCF identifies the relationship between observer dependency, enforcement dependency, and structural stability in complex systems. Eric Whitney subsequently applied the OCF framework to clinical medicine, formalizing a parallel principle for human-scale systems through the Encoded Human Project, and provided the cross-domain validation that the principle describes a real property of complex adaptive systems rather than a domain-specific observation.

The governance architecture application — recognizing that democratic norms are high observer dependency structures and that good governance design minimizes observer dependency in load-bearing functions — represents an independent convergence on the same structural principle through institutional analysis.

The "painting lines vs. pouring concrete" distinction in TSB's body of work is the preventive formulation: designing for intrinsic stability before norms break, rather than converting norms to concrete after they fail.
