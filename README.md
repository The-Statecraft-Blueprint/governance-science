# Governance Science

**Building professional infrastructure for democratic institutional design**

---

## What This Is

This repository contains the foundational infrastructure for governance science as a professional discipline:

- **Domain Specific Language (DSL)**: A formal glossary of 50-100 terms for precise communication about governance architecture
- **Design Pattern Library**: Documented, proven approaches to recurring governance challenges
- **Reference Implementations**: Real-world examples demonstrating patterns in action across different democracies
- **Comprehensive Framework**: How these pieces fit together to form a profession

**Goal**: Establish governance architecture as a recognized field—with common vocabulary, professional standards, academic rigor, and practical utility—similar to how engineering, medicine, or law function as established professions.

---

## Why This Matters

Democracies currently have no systematic way to:
- Diagnose structural governance problems
- Design evidence-based solutions
- Implement improvements professionally  
- Learn from what works and what doesn't
- Maintain institutional knowledge across administrations

**Result**: Government systems accumulate "design debt." Path dependency locks in obsolete architecture. Structural problems persist regardless of electoral outcomes.

**What's missing**: Professional capacity for institutional design.

This repository builds that professional capacity.

## The Three Foundations

### 1. Domain Specific Language (DSL)

**The Problem**: Scattered practitioners can't find each other because they don't share vocabulary. Economists say "mechanism design," political scientists say "institutional design," engineers say "systems architecture"—they're working on the same problems but can't communicate.

**The Solution**: A formal, precise vocabulary that creates a Schelling point where practitioners recognize each other.

Examples:
- **Governance Architecture**: The institutional rules and structures that shape how decisions are made (distinct from policy)
- **Structural Selection Pressure**: How system design rewards certain behaviors regardless of individual intent
- **Bounded Delegation**: Granting professional authority within democratically-defined boundaries

### 2. Design Pattern Library

**The Problem**: Every governance designer reinvents solutions to recurring challenges.

**The Solution**: Document proven patterns—like design patterns in software engineering or building architecture—so practitioners can adapt rather than reinvent.

Example: **Bounded Delegation Pattern**
- *Intent*: Enable professional expertise while maintaining democratic accountability
- *Structure*: Democratic body sets goals → Professional body designs implementation → Evaluation ensures alignment → Override preserves democratic control
- *Known Uses*: Federal Reserve, Supreme Court, New Zealand Electoral Commission

### 3. The Democratic Implementation Engine (DIE)

**The Problem**: Even when we identify structural flaws and design solutions, democracies have no professional capacity to implement them.

**The Solution**: A professional body whose job is governance architecture—separate from the politicians who make policy within those structures.

The DIE is the abstract concept. Implementation varies by country's legal and constitutional framework.

## Standards and Rigor

All work in this repository maintains:

**Academic Rigor**:
- Precise definitions
- Cited evidence
- Peer-reviewable claims
- Builds on established theory (institutional economics, mechanism design, systems theory)

**Practical Utility**:
- Practitioners can apply the patterns
- Concrete examples throughout
- Trade-offs explicitly acknowledged
- Implementation guidance provided

**International Applicability**:
- Not "fix America" but professional capacity for democracies globally
- Evidence from multiple contexts
- Adaptable frameworks

## The Long Game

**This is cathedral work.** Establishing a profession takes 10-25 years, not months.

**Vision**: When democracies worldwide face governance challenges, they should turn to certified governance scientists the same way they turn to engineers for infrastructure, doctors for health systems, or lawyers for legal frameworks.

"We need a governance architect" should be as normal as "we need an engineer."

That profession doesn't exist yet. This repository builds the infrastructure that makes it possible.
