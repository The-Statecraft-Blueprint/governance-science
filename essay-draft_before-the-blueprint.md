# Before the Blueprint: Building the Vocabulary of Governance Science

*Draft v2 — The Statecraft Blueprint | May 2026*

---

Six months ago, this publication ran an essay called "Refactoring Government: Why America Needs Governance Architects." If you missed it, that's fine — it didn't travel far. It was one of TSB's earliest pieces, written before the voice had developed and before the methodology had clarified. The ideas in it were right. The problem was that they were abstract in a way that gave readers nothing to hold onto. The Governance Design Agency, governance architects, governance science — these are all valid concepts. But they are words for things that do not exist yet, describing a field with no established tools, no shared language, no demonstrated practice. The essay hoped readers would get the gist. Most didn't, because the gist was invisible.

This is the problem the original essay didn't know it had: before you can explain what governance architects would do, you have to establish that governance architecture is a thing — a real discipline with its own methods, its own vocabulary, its own accumulated knowledge. Without that foundation, the proposal floats. It sounds like a good idea in the way that "we should design things more carefully" sounds like a good idea. True, but not actionable. Not yet a field.

This essay is about what comes before the field. Before the institutions, before the professionals, before the certifications. Before the blueprint.

---

Every serious discipline — every field of inquiry that has moved from observation to practice — develops its own vocabulary. This is not gatekeeping. It is how knowledge becomes transferable.

Physics has *work*. Not effort, not labor, but force times distance in a specific direction — a quantity that can be calculated, compared, and built upon by any physicist anywhere. The everyday word "work" carries too many meanings. Too much ambiguity bleeds into the conversation, and precision gets lost. The technical definition eliminates the ambiguity. A physicist in Tokyo and a physicist in Nairobi are discussing exactly the same thing, without first negotiating what they mean.

Law has *standing*. Not just the right to be in court, but a precisely defined set of conditions — injury in fact, causation, redressability — without which a case is dismissed regardless of its merits. Any trained attorney hears "standing" and knows immediately what question is being asked, what the threshold is, and what it means if the answer is no. That compression is not jargon for its own sake. It is precision in service of a field where ambiguity has consequences.

Medicine has a vocabulary for anatomy that works the same way. "Your calf hurts" could be the gastrocnemius, the soleus, the plantaris, or referred pain from somewhere else entirely. These are different structures with different injury mechanisms, different treatments, and different recovery timelines. "Medial gastrocnemius strain" takes three words, names the specific muscle, the specific side of it, and the type of injury. A clinician hearing those three words has a complete clinical picture. A clinician hearing "calf pain" has to start a diagnostic process from scratch.

What all these vocabularies share: they compress precise meaning into portable form. Expertise becomes transmissible. Knowledge accumulates rather than being rediscovered. A practitioner who encounters a new instance of a known pattern can recognize it immediately — apply the existing analysis, know what the diagnostic or design implications are — without rebuilding the entire conceptual framework from the beginning every time.

There is a cost, and it deserves honest acknowledgment. Specialized vocabulary creates barriers. Most of us don't know what "medial gastrocnemius" means without looking it up. When we talk to our doctors, the vocabulary gap is real and sometimes frustrating. Your doctor knows this. They use precise clinical vocabulary with colleagues — because precision matters when the decisions affect your health — and then translate it into plain language for you, because you are not their colleague and should not have to be. The translation is not a failure of the vocabulary. It is how the vocabulary and the broader public coexist.

This is the architecture that every mature field maintains: a technical layer for practitioners, and a translation layer for everyone else. The vocabulary makes the technical layer rigorous. The translation makes the insights from that layer available without requiring everyone to learn the terms. You don't need to know what "medial gastrocnemius" means to understand that your calf injury needs a specific treatment. You need your doctor to know — and to be able to communicate with the next doctor, and the researcher who documented the injury mechanism, and the physical therapist who will manage the recovery, all with precision and without starting from scratch each time.

---

This is why TSB uses so many metaphors and analogies.

The patterns in how governance fails are real and visible once you know what you're looking at. But for most readers, they are invisible — not because readers are inattentive, but because no shared vocabulary exists to point at them. So the work has been to find analogies that help people see what the vocabulary would eventually name precisely. Gravity. Basketball rulebooks. Load-bearing walls. Each analogy is a translation of a structural observation that does not yet have a precise term. Each one approximates the concept without capturing it exactly. That is what analogies do — and it is also their limitation.

When a field has no vocabulary, knowledge stays with the observer. It cannot travel. It cannot accumulate. It cannot be built upon. Each analyst begins from the beginning, re-describes what previous analysts have already described, and produces no cumulative discipline — only a collection of observations that cannot speak to each other. This is not a complaint. It is a design problem. The solution is vocabulary.

---

To be clear about where governance science actually is right now: the field does not exist. There are no practitioners beyond a handful of people thinking along adjacent lines without a shared framework. There is no institution, no curriculum, no professional community. Right now, the terms being developed here will be used primarily in technical analysis — in Church Bells briefs, in detailed structural assessments — while TSB's essays and posts will continue to translate those findings into plain language for general readers. The formal vocabulary is not meant for everyone. It is meant for the people who want to do this work rigorously, the ones who will eventually constitute the field.

The hope is that those people exist and will find their way here. That as governance architecture becomes a recognized area of inquiry, practitioners will arrive who want a shared shorthand — who would rather write "this system exhibits a dominant extractive pattern" than re-explain the entire mechanism from scratch in every brief. That the vocabulary, once built, will attract the people who need it.

For readers who are not those people: the essays and the translations will continue. The vocabulary does not require you to learn it to benefit from it. It requires the people doing the technical work to have it — so their analysis is precise, cumulative, and buildable — and then to translate what they find for everyone else. That is the architecture. That is how every mature field works.

---

For the past several months, the foundational layer has been under construction. The governance-science repository at [github.com/The-Statecraft-Blueprint/governance-science](https://github.com/The-Statecraft-Blueprint/governance-science/tree/main) is a public record of that effort: a domain-specific language for governance architecture, and a growing library of documented design patterns for recurring governance challenges.

The domain-specific language currently includes formal definitions of terms like *structure* (the how of governance — the rules and arrangements that shape how decisions get made, as distinct from the what of policy and the who of personnel), *degeneracy* (the property of an accountability mechanism that cannot distinguish between good and bad decisions, regardless of how much effort goes into enforcement), *structural drift* (why emergency powers expand across administrations regardless of which party built them), *observer dependency* (why mechanisms that depend on actors choosing to observe them collapse when someone stops choosing), and *dominant extractive pattern* (the formal criterion for when a system's architecture routes outcomes toward concentrated interests by design rather than by accident — a threshold, not just an observation).

The pattern library documents recurring design challenges alongside the structural approaches that address them, formatted the way software engineering has documented design patterns since the 1990s, adapted for institutional design. Patterns like *consequence alignment* and *actionable transparency* — the precise distinction between disclosure that relieves political pressure and disclosure that actually changes behavior.

None of this is finished. The foundation is real.

---

Two terms are worth showing in action, because the demonstration is the point.

*Observer dependency* is the structural explanation for something TSB has argued since early in this project: that democracy should not require heroic effort. That a system depending on exceptional individuals, constant vigilance, and continuous political will to function is fragile by design — not because of the people, but because of the architecture.

The clearest current example is not a historical norm but a live one. The executive toolkit — war powers, emergency economic authorities, surveillance infrastructure — has been built up across administrations and is now loaded and available. The conventional response to concern about that toolkit is: make sure only the right people get near it. Stay vigilant. Pay attention. Vote carefully. All of that is true. It is also a description of a high-observer-dependency system: one that functions only as long as enough people are paying close enough attention to prevent misuse, and that degrades the moment that vigilance lapses.

The structural question is different: why does the architecture permit the toolkit to be loaded in the first place? A mechanism with low observer dependency functions correctly regardless of who operates it — not because good people are always in charge, but because the design constrains what anyone in charge is able to do. Sunset clauses. Legislative reauthorization requirements. Automatic expiration. These are the structural alternatives to permanent vigilance. The term makes the design question precise: what would it take to reduce the observer dependency of this mechanism? That question cannot be asked without the vocabulary to name the property.

*Dominant extractive pattern* gives precise language to the diagnosis TSB made in one of its earliest essays, "Legislative Coercion." When a system systematically routes costs to most of its participants while concentrating benefits among those with proximity to power — and when this routing is architectural rather than incidental — it exhibits a dominant extractive pattern. The formal threshold: when 60% or more of a system's operational pattern is extractive, the minority that achieves the system's stated purpose does not constitute evidence that the system is working. The dominant pattern is the design.

Members of Congress spend more than thirty hours a week in call centers dialing wealthy donors. The architecture routes legislators' time and responsiveness toward concentrated donor interests, and it does this regardless of individual legislators' values or intentions. The people who serve their constituents genuinely are the 40% or less that the dominant pattern permits through. Their existence does not mean the system is working. It means the extraction is not total.

The practical implication is the most important one: a system exhibiting a dominant extractive pattern cannot be repaired by replacing the people inside it. The architecture will route the same incentives to whoever takes their place. Personnel replacement treats the symptom while the dominant pattern continues. "Better systems, not better people" is the plain-language version. The term makes the diagnostic threshold precise — and makes the design implication unavoidable.

---

The concepts in this vocabulary were not invented in isolation. The same structural principles have arrived independently from a systems analyst working on institutional collapse risk and from a clinical physician applying the framework to human behavioral patterns. Different domains, different methods, same structural finding. When the same principle surfaces across fields that have no prior conversation with each other, it is evidence that the principle describes something real — that this is physics, not philosophy.

---

The original essay called for a Governance Design Agency, university programs, professional certifications. Those remain the destination. They are decades away, best case. They always were.

Before you can have a profession, you need a field. Before you can have a field, you need the ability to say what you mean with precision — and have the next person who cares about this work understand exactly what you said.

The vocabulary stage is not glamorous. It does not look like an agency or a credential. It looks like a GitHub repository with formal definitions and documented patterns, maintained by people who believe that rigor is the first requirement of any serious attempt to build something that lasts.

Fields do not begin with institutions. They begin with language.

The work has started. [Here is where it lives.](https://github.com/The-Statecraft-Blueprint/governance-science/tree/main)
