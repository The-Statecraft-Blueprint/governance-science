*Drafted 2026-09-02. Two runs, split by analytic purpose rather than by jurisdiction count.*

## Which jurisdictions, and why these

**Yes, more than New Zealand — but chosen to vary, not to accumulate.** The purpose of this research is to find out **which parts of the framework are U.S.-specific**, and you only learn that from cases that differ along the axis you care about. Four cases, each earning its place:

| Case | What it varies | Which slot it tests | Why now |
|---|---|---|---|
| **New Zealand** — Building Act 1991 → leaky homes | Westminster system; the existing load-bearing case | 1 + 2 present, 3–5 absent | Post-mortem is unpinned; the Westminster caveat has never been checked |
| **United Kingdom** — post-Grenfell / Hackitt Review | Jurisdiction, **holding the domain constant** | Same slots, second instance | The cleanest experimental design available: same domain, different country, isolates the jurisdiction variable |
| **Sweden** — preparatory works + the *ministerstyre* prohibition | Legal family (civil law), constitutional structure | **Slot 1**, possibly slots 2–3 | The strongest candidate for the separation already existing in constitutional law — and it is not in the corpus at all |
| **Switzerland** — Federal Constitution Art. 170 | Maximum distance from the U.S. — direct democracy, collegial executive, no constitutional review of federal statutes | **Slot 5** | "If it works, it is the best precedent. If it is dead letter, that matters before citing it." |

**Deliberately excluded:** EU Art. 296 TFEU and Australia's *Acts Interpretation Act* s.15AA. Both are already verified as text and neither needs a research run — they need a citation check, which is a five-minute job, not a Deep Research question.

**A specific thing to watch for in Run 2.** Sweden constitutionally prohibits *ministerstyre* — ministerial interference in an agency's handling of individual cases — with the government setting objectives and appropriations through *regleringsbrev* (annual appropriation directions) while agencies decide implementation independently. If that is accurate, **it is a mandate/architecture separation operating at constitutional level, continuously, for centuries.** It is potentially the single most important prior art for the whole framework and it appears nowhere in the corpus. Treat this paragraph as `[Unverified]` — it is a prompt for the research, not a finding.

## RUN 1 — Performance-based building regulation as a natural experiment

> I am testing an analytic framework about why regulatory systems fail to detect their own failures, and I need a rigorous, source-grounded comparison of two building-regulation cases. **I want disconfirmation at least as much as confirmation — please actively look for evidence that the framing below is wrong, and say so plainly if you find it.**
>
> **The analytic grid I am testing.** I hypothesise that a functioning regulatory system needs five distinct things, and that failures can be located by identifying which are missing:
> 1. A **stated objective** — written down somewhere authoritative, in terms that make achievement determinable after the fact.
> 2. A **checkable design** — the implementing rules can be shown to serve that objective, against alternatives, on a record.
> 3. **Checkable execution** — anyone can determine whether the design was actually carried out.
> 4. A **report channel** — a person who encounters a failure can get it somewhere that registers.
> 5. A **correction path** — the design can be revised without reopening the underlying political settlement.
>
> **Case A — New Zealand, Building Act 1991 and the leaky homes crisis.**
> - What exactly did the 1991 Act change? Was the shift to performance-based regulation a change at the level of the statute, the Building Code, the approval process, or all three? Be precise about which layer moved, because the distinction matters to my argument.
> - What did the authoritative post-mortems actually conclude about causation? I am specifically looking for the **Hunn Report (2002)**, the **Building Industry Authority** reports, any Law Commission or parliamentary inquiry material, and the peer-reviewed retrospectives. **Give me exact document titles, authors, dates and page or section references I can cite.**
> - I have seen the claim, attributed to a post-mortem, that *"monitoring and feedback loops to check the ongoing achievement of performance standards for innovative solutions were not designed into the system."* **Find the primary source of that sentence or something close to it, and tell me if it does not exist.**
> - How do the careful sources apportion causation among performance-based regulation, the deregulation of the building trades, untreated timber, monolithic cladding design, and the loss of apprenticeship-based skills? I want the balanced scholarly account, not the popular one.
> - What was subsequently changed — the Building Act 2004, the licensed building practitioner regime, product-assurance rules — and **is there any evidence about whether those changes worked?**
>
> **Case B — United Kingdom, Grenfell Tower and the Hackitt Review.**
> - The UK also ran a performance-based ("functional requirements") building regime under the Building Regulations and Approved Documents. What were the actual mechanics — how were functional requirements meant to be demonstrated, and by whom?
> - What did **Dame Judith Hackitt's *Building a Safer Future* (2018)** conclude about the regulatory system as a system, as distinct from conclusions about any particular product or actor? I am interested in her findings about whether anyone was checking that buildings performed as intended, and about where accountability sat.
> - What did the **Grenfell Tower Inquiry Phase 2 report** conclude about the regulatory and testing regime — again, the systemic findings rather than the findings about individual firms?
> - How did the **Building Safety Act 2022** change the feedback and accountability architecture? What is the evidence so far on whether it is working?
>
> **The comparison I actually need.**
> - Both countries adopted outcome-based building regulation and both had a systemic failure. **Were these the same kind of failure, or do they only look alike?** Where do the mechanisms genuinely differ?
> - **Is it fair to say either system separated "the objective" from "the implementation design" in a way analogous to a legislature stating a goal and a separate expert body designing how to meet it?** Or is performance-based building regulation a different thing that shares vocabulary with that idea? Push back on me here if the analogy is loose.
> - New Zealand and the UK are both Westminster systems with executives that command the legislature, no strong separation of powers in the U.S. sense, and (for NZ) no codified constitution. **Does that system type do explanatory work in either failure?** Would the same regulatory design have failed differently in a separation-of-powers system, and is there any comparative literature that addresses this?
> - Is there any jurisdiction that adopted performance-based building regulation and **did** build a verification loop, with evidence about the outcome? A positive case would be more valuable to me than a third failure.
>
> **Sourcing standard.** Primary documents and peer-reviewed work wherever possible; name the source for every substantive claim. Distinguish clearly between (a) what a document says, (b) what commentators say it says, and (c) your own inference. Where the evidence is thin or contested, say so rather than smoothing it over. If a claim I have made above is simply wrong, lead with that.

## RUN 2 — Stated objectives and evaluation duties in Sweden and Switzerland

> I am researching whether any democracy has institutionalised two things that the United States largely has not: **(a) writing down what a law is supposed to achieve, in a place that carries authority, and (b) an obligation to check afterwards whether it did.** I want a rigorous, source-grounded account of how Sweden and Switzerland handle each. **Please look for evidence that cuts against my framing as well as for it, and tell me where I am overreaching.**
>
> **Background on why I am asking.** In U.S. practice, statutes are typically written as procedures — what shall be done — without stating an evaluable objective, so whether a law achieved anything is often unanswerable by construction. I want to know which democracies have a structural answer to this, how it works in practice rather than on paper, and what it costs.
>
> **Sweden — three questions.**
>
> 1. **Preparatory works (*förarbeten*).** What exactly are they — *utredningar*/SOU, *propositioner*, committee reports — and what is their actual legal status? How do Swedish courts and agencies use them, and how binding are they in practice? **Critically: do they in fact contain statements of what legislation is meant to achieve, in terms specific enough that someone could later determine whether it worked? Or do they mainly record reasoning and compromise?** That distinction is the whole question for me, so please be concrete and give examples of actual preparatory-works language.
> 2. **The *ministerstyre* prohibition.** Sweden constitutionally restricts ministers from intervening in individual agency decisions, with the government instead steering agencies through appropriation directions (*regleringsbrev*) and objectives. **Is my understanding right?** How does this work concretely — what does a *regleringsbrev* actually contain, how specific are the objectives, and who checks whether they were met? How old is this arrangement, and what is the scholarly assessment of whether it functions as intended or is circumvented informally?
> 3. **Evaluation.** What does the *Riksrevisionen* (National Audit Office) do, and is there anything resembling a standing duty to evaluate whether laws achieved their stated purposes? What has that evaluation actually produced, and does anything happen as a result?
>
> **Switzerland — three questions.**
>
> 1. **Article 170 of the Federal Constitution** ("The Federal Assembly shall ensure that the efficacy of measures taken by the Confederation is evaluated"). I have verified the text. **I have no idea whether it does anything.** What is the implementing legislation and practice? How was it interpreted after its 1999/2000 adoption?
> 2. **The Parliamentary Control of the Administration (PCA/*Parlamentarische Verwaltungskontrolle*) and the Control Committees.** What do they actually produce — how many evaluations, of what kind, at what depth? **What happens to the findings?** Is there evidence of laws or implementations actually being changed as a result, or is this a reporting function that terminates in a report?
> 3. **The scholarly assessment.** There is a Swiss evaluation-research literature (Bussmann, Balthasar, Widmer, Sager and others). **What does it conclude about whether Art. 170 changed anything?** I want the critical assessments especially — if the consensus is that it is largely symbolic, that is more useful to me than a favourable summary.
>
> **The cross-cutting questions that matter most to me.**
> - Both countries differ enormously from the U.S. — Sweden has a dualist executive with independent agencies and a parliamentary system; Switzerland has direct democracy, a collegial executive, and no constitutional review of federal statutes. **How much of what each does is portable to a separation-of-powers system, and how much depends on the surrounding constitutional structure?** Be specific about what would not travel.
> - Is there evidence in **either** country that stating objectives up front actually changes outcomes — better implementation, faster correction, failures caught earlier? Or is the honest answer that nobody has measured it?
> - **Where a stated objective exists but no correction mechanism follows, what happens?** I suspect the objective alone accomplishes little, and I would like evidence either way.
> - Are there other democracies with a stronger claim than these two that I should be looking at instead? Name them and say why.
>
> **Sourcing standard.** Primary legal texts (with article numbers), official reports (with document numbers), and peer-reviewed scholarship. Where you rely on secondary characterisation, say so. **Distinguish rigorously between what the law provides for and what demonstrably happens** — that gap is the subject of the research, and a summary that blurs it is worse than no answer. Non-English sources are expected and welcome; give original titles alongside translations.

## When the results come back

Two things to do before the material is used anywhere, both from the corpus's own rules:

1. **Label everything.** Deep Research output is `[Unverified]` by default no matter how confident it reads. It becomes `[Documented]` only when the primary source has been opened. The Nordic preparatory-works claim has already sat at "believed, checked only lightly" for months — this is the pass that fixes it or kills it.
2. **Write down the negative results.** If Art. 170 turns out to be dead letter, or if *ministerstyre* is routinely circumvented, or if the NZ post-mortem sentence cannot be sourced — those go in `RESEARCH_LEADS.md` §2 the same day. A dead end recorded once beats a dead end walked three times, and a negative result here is genuinely valuable: **"nobody has built slot 5 and made it work" is a finding, not a failure.**

And one thing to resist: Deep Research is agreeable by construction. If both runs come back broadly confirming the five-slot grid, **that is weak evidence, not strong** — the prompts asked for disconfirmation, so an absence of it should prompt a second look rather than confidence.
